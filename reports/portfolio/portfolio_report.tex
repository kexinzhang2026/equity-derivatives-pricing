\kicker{Abstract}\par\vspace{3pt}
\small
A Fourier (COS), a Crank--Nicolson and a Monte Carlo engine are built and checked against each other and
against published reference values on three test contracts. The validated library is then calibrated to
end-of-day SPX option quotes. One Heston parameter set fits the 2023-01-18 surface to 0.89 implied-vol points,
with its largest errors at one month; on the same surface $\kappa$ is only weakly identified, and across 52
weekly fits in 2023 its changes move with those of $\xi$ (correlation 0.96). For a 3-year autocallable, Heston and
Dupire local vol imply fair coupons of 15.2\% and 16.0\%. A control model with Heston's own vanilla prices but
local-vol dynamics attributes 0.77 of the 0.78-point gap to dynamics and 0.01 to calibration differences. In a
daily-hedged SPX backtest, an exact full-revaluation split shows that the 2023 profit came from realised
variance below implied ($+74.2$ points) partly offset by implied-vol re-marks ($-34.2$). It also shows that the
large residual of the Greek (Taylor) attribution came mainly from the last three days before expiry.

% ================================================================= 1 ARCHITECTURE
\section{Research Design}\label{sec:design}
\subsection{Sequence}
The project has four stages. Each stage uses only components that the previous stage has already validated,
because a pricing error in stage 1 would contaminate every later number.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Numerics.} Do Fourier, finite-difference and simulation methods give the same price for the same
contract, and does each converge at its theoretical rate \citep{fang2008,andersen2008,longstaff2001}?
\item \textbf{Calibration.} How well do Heston \citep{heston1993}, SABR \citep{hagan2002} and Dupire local vol
\citep{dupire1994} fit the SPX surface, and which parameters does the surface actually identify?
\item \textbf{Model risk.} When several models are fitted to the same surface, how far apart are their prices for
path-dependent products, and how much of the difference comes from calibration and how much from dynamics?
\item \textbf{Hedging.} Where does the P\&L of a delta-hedged option come from, and how much of it can the
Greeks explain on real data?
\end{enumerate}
Each stage has a check that does not depend on the market: a closed form, a published reference value, a limiting
case, or a synthetic surface whose true parameters are known. Real data enter only after a method has passed
on synthetic inputs, so a fit error on SPX can be read as a property of the model rather than of the code.

\subsection{Data and cleaning}
End-of-day SPX option quotes (16:00 ET) come from optionsDX for January--March 2020 (the COVID crash) and all of
2023 (a calm, rising market). Each row holds the call and put bid and ask at one strike and expiry, with the index
level. One loader maps optionsDX, OptionMetrics and Yahoo snapshots to a common schema. Filters, per quote date
and expiry:
\begin{itemize}
\item two-sided quotes (positive bid, ask above bid, mid at least 0.05), relative spread at most 50\%, at least 7
days to expiry;
\item standardised log-moneyness $\ln(K/F)/(0.2\sqrt{T})$ between $-4$ and $2.5$, where SPX quotes are liquid;
\item out-of-the-money options only: puts below the forward, calls above it.
\end{itemize}
The forward and discount factor are implied, not assumed. For strikes quoted on both sides, put--call parity gives
\begin{equation}
C(K) - P(K) = D\,(F - K),
\end{equation}
which is linear in $K$; a regression on near-the-money strikes gives slope $-D$ and intercept $DF$. No stale index
level, assumed dividend yield or external rate curve enters the surface.

\subsection{Models}
Under the risk-neutral measure with rate $r$ and dividend yield $q$, the Heston model is
\begin{equation}
\frac{dS_t}{S_t} = (r-q)\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,dW^S_t,\qquad
dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW^v_t,\qquad d\langle W^S,W^v\rangle_t = \rho\,dt .
\end{equation}
Its characteristic function is used in the ``little Heston trap'' form \citep{albrecher2007}, which avoids the
branch-cut discontinuity of the complex logarithm at long maturities. The COS method \citep{fang2008} expands the
density of $x=\ln(S_T/K)$ in a cosine series on a truncation interval $[a,b]$:
\begin{equation}
V_0 \approx e^{-rT}\sideset{}{'}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{Re}\!\left[\varphi\!\left(\frac{k\pi}{b-a}\right)
e^{-ik\pi\frac{a}{b-a}}\right] V_k ,
\end{equation}
where $\varphi$ is the characteristic function, $V_k$ are the cosine coefficients of the payoff and the prime halves
the first term. The interval comes from the cumulants of $x$, computed numerically from the cumulant generating
function.

Local volatility is computed from total implied variance $w(y,T)=\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2T$ at log-moneyness
$y=\ln(K/F)$ \citep{gatheral2006}:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{loc}}^2(y,T) = \frac{\partial_T w}{1 - \frac{y}{w}\partial_y w
+ \frac14\left(-\frac14 - \frac1w + \frac{y^2}{w^2}\right)(\partial_y w)^2 + \frac12\partial_{yy} w}.
\end{equation}
Each expiry is fitted with an SVI slice \citep{gatheral2014}, so the $y$-derivatives are analytic; the
$T$-derivative uses linear interpolation in total variance between slices.

\section{Three Engines, One Price}\label{sec:engines}
\subsection{Cross-validation}
Table~\ref{tab:xval} prices three test contracts with every applicable engine. They are different contracts,
chosen because each has a trusted reference:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Black-Scholes call:} $S=100$, $K=95$, $T=0.75$, $r=4\%$, $q=1.5\%$, $\sigma=25\%$.
\item \textbf{Heston call:} the \citet{fang2008} test case, $S=K=100$, $T=1$, $r=q=0$, with $v_0=0.0175$,
$\kappa=1.5768$, $\theta=0.0398$, $\xi=0.5751$, $\rho=-0.5711$. The Feller condition is violated, so the variance
process reaches zero: the hard case for simulation, handled by the Andersen quadratic-exponential scheme
\citep{andersen2008}. The published reference value is 5.785155450.
\item \textbf{American put:} the \citet{longstaff2001} test case, $S=36$, $K=40$, $T=1$, $r=6\%$, $\sigma=20\%$.
\end{itemize}

Agreement is judged against each method's own error: the PDE value differs from the closed form by its
discretisation error ($10^{-4}$), the Monte Carlo values lie within 0.3 standard errors of the reference for the two
European contracts, and COS matches the published Heston value to $2\times10^{-8}$ and the \citet{lewis2001} integral
to $10^{-9}$. All rows satisfy the no-arbitrage bounds of their own contracts.

For the American put, Longstaff--Schwartz uses a Laguerre basis on 50 exercise dates, with the exercise rule fitted
on one set of paths and applied to an independent set. An out-of-sample rule is sub-optimal by construction, so its
value is a lower bound for the 50-date Bermudan, which is itself worth less than the American. The Monte Carlo value
lies below the PDE value, as it must.

\begin{callout}
\textbf{Published benchmarks can be wrong.} The finite-difference value 4.478 quoted for this put in
\citet{longstaff2001} is 0.009 below the value from both a 20,000-step binomial tree and the Crank--Nicolson
solver (4.4867). Without a second engine the discrepancy would have looked like a bug in the PDE.
\end{callout}

\subsection{Convergence}
Crank--Nicolson is second order in space and time only if the payoff kink does not pollute the solution. The
payoff is averaged over the grid cell containing the strike, and the first time steps are fully implicit half-steps
\citep[Rannacher start-up;][]{rannacher1984}. The Crank--Nicolson amplification factor tends to $-1$ for stiff
modes, so the high-frequency error created by the kink would otherwise never be damped.

Two further results come from the exotics. A knock-out barrier that falls between grid nodes reduces the PDE to
first order; aligning the grid so that the barrier is a node restores second order. For the autocallable the value
jumps at the autocall level and at the knock-in barrier; giving each discontinuity node the average of its two
one-sided values restores fast convergence, and the PDE matches Monte Carlo within one standard error.

% ================================================================= 3 CALIBRATION
\section{Calibration to the SPX Surface}\label{sec:calib}
\subsection{Objective and synthetic check}
The calibration minimises vega-weighted price errors,
\begin{equation}
\min_{\Theta}\ \sum_i \left(\frac{C^{\text{model}}_i(\Theta) - C^{\text{mkt}}_i}{\max(\mathcal{V}_i,\ \underline{\mathcal{V}})}\right)^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{V}_i$ is the Black-Scholes vega and the floor $\underline{\mathcal{V}}$ is 1\% of the median vega. To
first order each term is an implied-vol error, so the objective is in vol points without a root-finding step inside
the optimiser. A bounded trust-region least-squares solver runs from several starting points. On synthetic Heston
surfaces the calibrator recovers the true parameters to $10^{-4}$; with 0.2 vol points of quote noise added, the fit
stops at the noise level (RMSE 0.16\vp).

The fitted Heston parameters are $v_0=0.039$, $\kappa=1.89$, $\theta=0.072$, $\xi=0.97$, $\rho=-0.71$ (Feller

needs a vol of vol that the condition does not allow.

\end{figure}

\begin{callout}
\textbf{A model limitation, most likely not an optimiser failure.} In Heston the short-dated skew comes only from the
correlated variance diffusion, which needs time to act, so the model struggles to produce the steep one-month SPX
skew without an extreme vol of vol. All four optimiser starts reach the same minimum on this date, and in 2023 only 3
of 52 weekly fits had a start ending more than 1\% worse. That makes an optimiser failure unlikely, though it does not
exclude every effect of the parameter bounds. The conclusion is about Heston: local vol, also a diffusion, fits the
one-month slice to 0.43\vp. The pattern is the usual motivation for jumps \citep{bates1996} or rough volatility.
\end{callout}

SABR fits better for a different reason. Its vol of vol $\nu$ falls from 2.25 at one month to 0.58 at two years,
roughly as $T^{-0.45}$: the term structure is absorbed into parameters that change with maturity, so SABR interpolates
each slice rather than describing the dynamics of the whole surface.

\subsection{Local volatility}
Dupire local vol built from the SVI slices reproduces a synthetic surface to 0.03\vp. On the real surface it reprices
the stage 3 option set to 0.61\vp{} (Section~\ref{sec:modelrisk}); most of that is the SVI fit itself, 0.3--0.4\vp{}
per slice, with the rest from interpolation between slices and from using constant $r$ and $q$. Its at-the-money skew
is 1.95 times the implied skew, close to the short-maturity rule that implied vol at strike $K$ is roughly the average
of local vol between spot and $K$.

% ================================================================= 4 IDENTIFIABILITY
\section{Parameter Identifiability and Stability}\label{sec:ident}
\subsection{What a single surface identifies}
A good fit says nothing about whether each parameter is pinned down. The direct test is a profile: hold $\kappa$ fixed
on a grid, refit the other four parameters, and record the fit error. It was run first on synthetic surfaces, where
the answer is known, and then on the real SPX surface of 2023-01-18.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering

Holding $\kappa$ anywhere between 1.2 and 3.0 raises the RMSE by at most 0.1\vp, about one median half spread, while the

0.8\vp{} at $\kappa=10$. So $\kappa$ is \emph{weakly} identified, to roughly a factor of 2.5, with $\xi$ compensating along
the valley. The synthetic profile (Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}) shows the same shape, flatter when only short
expiries are used.

\end{itemize}

\begin{caution}
\textbf{Practical consequence.} Risk measures that depend on $\kappa$ separately from $\xi$, such as sensitivity to the
speed of mean reversion, are poorly determined, and daily recalibration feeds that noise into P\&L. Common remedies
are to fix $\kappa$ and recalibrate the rest, or to penalise changes from the previous day's parameters.
\end{caution}

% ================================================================= 5 MODEL RISK
\section{Exotics and Model Risk}\label{sec:modelrisk}
\subsection{Products and checks}
\textbf{Down-and-in barrier put.} The continuously monitored price has a closed form \citep{reiner1991}. Daily
monitoring is handled by the \citet{broadie1997} correction, which shifts the barrier away from spot by
$e^{-\beta\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ with $\beta = -\zeta(1/2)/\sqrt{2\pi}\approx 0.5826$. For the 3-year 100/60 put the corrected
closed form gives 4.92\% of spot; daily-monitored Monte Carlo gives $4.93 \pm 0.04$.

\textbf{Autocallable.} A 3-year note on SPX, notional 100, quarterly observations. If the index is at or above its initial
level on an observation date, the note redeems at par plus an 8\% p.a.\ accrued coupon. If it is never called and the
index has ever closed below 60\% (daily knock-in), the holder receives $100\,S_T/S_0$ when $S_T<S_0$, otherwise par. It is
priced by Monte Carlo and by two coupled PDEs (knocked in and not knocked in) stepped once per business day: under
Black-Scholes the PDE gives 97.18 against $97.20 \pm 0.04$ by Monte Carlo, and under local vol both give 96.63. Because the
present value is affine in the coupon, $PV(c) = A + cB$, the fair coupon is $c^\ast = (100 - A)/B$.

\subsection{Calibration for a 3-year product}
The stage 2 calibration stops at 1.9 years, so stage 3 recalibrates on 7 expiries from one month to 2.92 years (170
options), the longest listed expiry on that date. This gives Heston parameters $v_0=0.039$, $\kappa=1.75$, $\theta=0.071$,
$\xi=0.96$, $\rho=-0.71$, close to the stage 2 fit. A single $r=3.93\%$ and $q=1.49\%$ are fitted to the implied discount and
forward curves. Beyond 2.92 years local vol holds each strike's implied vol constant, while Heston extrapolates through its
dynamics; the note's last month therefore depends on extrapolation. Black-Scholes uses the at-the-money implied vol at 2.92
years, 20.65\%.

\subsection{Do the models price the same vanillas?}
Fitting to the same surface does not mean fitting it equally well, so every option in the calibration set was repriced
under each model, local vol by Crank--Nicolson.

% ================================================================= 6 HEDGING
\section{Greeks and the Sources of Hedging P\&L}\label{sec:hedging}
\subsection{Greeks by simulation}
Monte Carlo Greeks were implemented three ways. The pathwise estimator differentiates along each path,
$\Delta = \mathbb{E}[e^{-rT}\mathbf{1}\{S_T>K\}S_T/S_0]$, and fails for a digital option, whose payoff has zero derivative almost
everywhere. The likelihood-ratio estimator moves the derivative onto the density,
$\Delta = \mathbb{E}[e^{-rT}f(S_T)\,Z/(S_0\sigma\sqrt T)]$, and works for any payoff at the cost of higher variance. A mixed estimator,
likelihood ratio applied to the pathwise delta, gives gamma. All three match the closed forms within three standard errors.

\subsection{The hedged P\&L identity}
For a short option that is delta-hedged and self-financed, the Black-Scholes PDE turns theta and financing into
$\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2\,dt$, so to second order one day's P\&L is
\begin{equation}
d\Pi \approx \underbrace{\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\left(\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2\,dt - \left(\tfrac{dS}{S}\right)^2\right)}_{\text{gamma/theta}}
\;-\; \underbrace{\mathcal{V}\,d\sigma_{\text{imp}}}_{\text{vega}}
\;-\; \underbrace{\left(\text{vanna}\cdot dS\,d\sigma_{\text{imp}} + \tfrac12\text{volga}\cdot d\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2\right)}_{\text{vanna/volga}}
\;+\;\varepsilon .
\end{equation}
A short-gamma position earns when realised variance is below implied and loses when it is above.

\subsection{Simulation: the cost of discrete hedging}
In a Black-Scholes world with a 3-month at-the-money call and $\sigma=20\%$, the hedging error from rebalancing $N$ times
should follow \citet{kamal1999}:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{sd}(\text{P\&L}) \approx \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\ \frac{\mathcal{V}\,\sigma}{\sqrt N}.
\end{equation}
With 63 daily rebalances the simulated standard deviation is 0.434, against 0.441 from the formula (per 100 notional). With
5 bp transaction costs, cost grows like $\sqrt N$ while hedging error falls like $1/\sqrt N$, giving an optimal frequency near
252 rebalances here.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{hedging_simulation.png}
\caption{Left: discrete-hedging error scales as $N^{-1/2}$ and matches Kamal--Derman. Centre: with 5 bp costs the RMS P\&L has a
minimum. Right: selling at 20\% implied when realised is 15\%; hedging with implied-vol and realised-vol deltas gives the same
mean (0.99) but different dispersion (0.35 vs 0.16).}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Real SPX backtest}
On the first trading day of each month one 1-month at-the-money SPX call is sold at mid (strike nearest the implied
forward, expiry nearest 30 days), hedged daily in the index, re-marked daily at mid, and closed on the last quote date
before expiry. Each day's P\&L is split two ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Taylor attribution:} the identity above, with Greeks at the start of the day.
\item \textbf{Full revaluation:} the option is repriced in three steps. (a) Time passes and the index moves at fixed implied
vol, with the forward rolling at constant carry; together with the hedge this is the realised-versus-implied variance term.
(b) The forward and discount factor move to their own day-end values: the forward--index basis. (c) Implied vol moves to its
day-end value. The three pieces add to the total exactly.
\end{itemize}

\begin{callout}
\textbf{Daily fit and cumulative attribution are different questions.} The Taylor terms explain 97\% of daily P\&L variance
in 2020 Q1 and 84\% in 2023 (91\% pooled, a figure dominated by the large 2020 days). Yet in 2023 the Taylor components sum to
$+0.1$ against a total of $+31.8$: almost the entire cumulative profit sits in the residual. Small errors with a consistent
sign accumulate even when the daily fit is good. The full revaluation removes the ambiguity.
\end{callout}

\textbf{Where the Taylor residual comes from.} Comparing each Taylor term with its full-revaluation counterpart locates the
2023 residual of $+31.7$ points:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Vol re-marks, $+39.4$.} Full revaluation gives $-34.2$ where vega plus vanna/volga give $-73.5$. Of this, $+25.0$
comes from the last three days before expiry. There, the fixed strike's implied vol changes by 5--25 vol points per day, and
vega itself changes sharply with both vol and time, so a second-order expansion in $d\sigma$ breaks down. Implied vols of
1--3 day options are also sensitive to the time convention near an AM-settled expiry.
\item \textbf{Forward--index basis, $-8.2$.} The ratio of the parity-implied forward to the reported index level moves by about
6 bp per day (standard deviation) against roughly 1 bp of carry. This is mostly asynchrony between the index print and the
option quotes. The term accounts for 15\% of daily P\&L variance in 2023 and is the main reason the daily fit is lower than in 2020.
\item \textbf{Spot and time, $+0.6$.} The gamma/theta Taylor term matches full revaluation closely in a calm year. In 2020 Q1 the
gap is $+11.7$, from third-order spot moves on large days.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Reading the backtest.} In 2023 implied vol averaged 15.6\% against 12.1\% realised, and the variance term earned
$+74.2$ points over 12 trades; implied-vol re-marks gave back $-34.2$. The re-mark loss is consistent with a fixed strike
sliding down the skew in a rising market, so that its implied vol rises even when the smile does not move. In 2020 Q1
realised vol of 36\% against 17\% implied cost $-84.7$ in the variance term and $-41.3$ in re-marks over three trades.

\begin{caution}
\textbf{Inference limits.} Fifteen trades in two chosen windows cannot estimate the return of selling volatility. The March
2020 trade ends after eight days at the data cut-off, so its outcome is not fully observed; the loss could have grown or
partly recovered. The backtest shows \emph{where} hedged P\&L came from, not how much a strategy would earn, and it ignores
bid--ask costs on the option and the hedge.
\end{caution}

\section{Evidence Summary}\label{sec:evidence}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Numerical correctness: strong, within scope.} Three engine types agree on the three test contracts within
discretisation or statistical error, match published or tree references, and converge at their theoretical rates.
\item \textbf{Heston's short-end misfit is a model limitation: strong for Heston.} Synthetic recovery to $10^{-4}$, stable
optimiser results and structured residuals point to the model rather than the fitting. It does not extend to other
diffusions.
\item \textbf{Weak identification of $\kappa$: strong on one surface, supported over the year.} The real-surface profile is flat
to within one half spread over $\kappa \in [1.2, 3.0]$; weekly $\kappa$ and $\xi$ changes correlate at 0.96.
\item \textbf{Heston--local vol coupon gap from dynamics: supportive.} A controlled experiment attributes $+0.77$ of $+0.78$
points to dynamics on one date and one note.
\item \textbf{Hedging attribution: descriptive.} The full-revaluation split is exact; the Taylor residual is located; the
economic reading rests on 15 trades.
\end{enumerate}
The contribution is not a new model. It is a library in which each number has an independent check, applied so that the
differences that remain, between models and between the hedge and the market, can be traced to a cause.

% ================================================================= 8 LIMITATIONS
\section{Limitations and Next Steps}
\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Data:} end-of-day mids from one vendor; the index level and the option quotes are not synchronous.
\item \textbf{Rates and dividends:} one $r$ and $q$ per date for local vol and the autocallable. The implied forward curve deviates
from this by up to about 50 bp across expiries.
\item \textbf{Fit quality:} no model reprices the surface inside the spread; local vol inherits the SVI slice errors.
\item \textbf{Heston PDE:} no 2D solver, so Heston prices are cross-checked by COS, Monte Carlo and a published value only.
\item \textbf{Scope:} the model-risk split uses one date and one product; the backtest uses 15 trades and no costs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Next steps, in priority order}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Repeat the calibration--dynamics split on the weekly 2023 surfaces, to see whether it holds over time.
\item Hedge with the parity-implied forward (or index futures) instead of the index print, removing the basis noise.
\item Close backtest trades several days before expiry, or attribute the final days separately, and add bid--ask costs.
\item Use term structures of $r$ and $q$ in local vol and in the autocallable.
\item Only then extend the model set: jumps for the short end, stochastic local vol for the autocallable.
\end{enumerate}
